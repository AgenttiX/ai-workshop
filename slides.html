Chat vs. harness

Chat

  • You copy code and errors in, you copy answers out
  • The model sees only what you paste
  • It cannot check the validity of its output
  • You are the loop

Harness

  • The model reads your files, runs your commands, edits, tests
  • It sees the actual code, data and errors
  • It verifies its own work with your tools
  • The loop runs itself; you review and steer

In our experience, a huge difference in what you can get done — and in what can go wrong. Hence this workshop.

What is a harness?

Harness (Claude Code, Codex, Copilot, Cursor, OpenCode, …)

Model  +  tools  +  context management  +  permission loop

  • Tools: read/edit files, run shell commands, search, web, MCP servers, subagents
  • Context management: what goes into the model's window: AGENTS.md, files, tool output, memory, compaction
  • Permission loop: the agent proposes an action, you (or your rules) approve it
read→ think→ act→ verify↺
The model is the same one you get in the chat app. The harness is what makes it an agent. The loop diagram comes back in the demo: every tool call is one turn of this loop.

Why file access and context matter

  • The model knows only what's in its context window (everything it sees at once): no memory between sessions unless the harness provides it
  • File access means the context is filled with the truth: your code, your data, the actual error message
  • Context is finite and degrades when full: the harness (and you) decide what goes in
    • AGENTS.md: the standing instructions, read every session
    • Tool outputs: what it read and ran
    • Compaction: summarizing the conversation so far when the window fills up
  • Rule of thumb: one task per session, clear when switching

Scaling laws: the loss is a power law

Loss \(L\): how badly the model predicts the next token (lower is better), as a function of the number of parameters \(N\) and of training tokens \(D\). Training compute \(C \approx 6ND\) floating-point operations.

\[ L(N,D) = E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}}, \qquad E = 1.82,\; A = 482,\; B = 2085,\; \alpha = 0.348,\; \beta = 0.366 \]
  • Minimizing \(L\) at fixed \(C\): ~20 tokens per parameter. Chinchilla (70B, 1.4T tokens) beat the 4× larger Gopher on the same compute
  • The original fit gave ~70 tokens per parameter; a 2024 replication traced this to an optimizer that stopped early
  • Smaller models get trained far beyond the optimum (Llama 3: "much longer than is compute-optimal"): they are cheaper to run

J. Hoffmann et al., arXiv:2203.15556 (2022). Fit: T. Besiroglu et al. (Epoch AI), Chinchilla scaling: a replication attempt, arXiv:2404.10102 (2024), eq. (3). Llama 3: arXiv:2407.21783.

Physicists will like this one: an empirical power law with an irreducible floor E (Hoffmann et al.: it "should correspond to the entropy of natural text") and two finite-size corrections. 6ND: 2 FLOP per parameter per token for the forward pass (one multiply-add), and the backward pass is "approximately twice the compute" (Kaplan et al. 2020, arXiv:2001.08361; used by Hoffmann et al.). It neglects the context-length term of attention. The constants on the slide are Epoch AI's refit (Besiroglu, Erdil, Barnett, You, 2024, eq. 3): 1.8172 + 482.01/N^0.3478 + 2085.43/D^0.3658, fitted to data they reconstructed from Hoffmann et al.'s Figure 4. Hoffmann et al.'s own published fit is E = 1.69, A = 406.4, B = 410.7, α = 0.34, β = 0.28 (their eq. 10). We recomputed the optimum of both fits ourselves (minimizing L at fixed C = 6ND): Epoch's gives 22 → 17 tokens per parameter for C = 10²¹ → 10²⁵ FLOP; Hoffmann et al.'s rounded fit gives 50 → 120. The replication: the intervals were "implausibly narrow"; one of the original authors confirmed the optimizer (L-BFGS-B) terminated early because the Huber losses were averaged instead of summed. The 20-tokens rule from their approaches 1 and 2 survives. Chinchilla: 70B parameters on 1.4T tokens, the same compute as Gopher (280B), and it "uniformly and significantly outperforms" Gopher, GPT-3 (175B) and others on their evaluations. Llama 3 405B: 15.6T tokens ≈ 39 per parameter, "approximately compute-optimal" by Meta's own scaling-law fit on their data. Meta: "we also train our smaller models for much longer than is compute-optimal. The resulting models perform better than compute-optimal models at the same inference budget." Caveat: the loss is not the ability. The power law is smooth; benchmark scores and task horizons are what people care about, and their link to the loss is less clean. The constants depend on the data, the tokenizer and the architecture; don't carry the numbers over to other models.

How the task horizon is measured, and its caveats

METR timed skilled professionals on software, ML and cybersecurity tasks (170 in the paper, 228 in the current suite) and ran AI agents on the same tasks. The 50 % time horizon \(h\): the human time at which the agent's fitted success rate is 50 %.

\[ p_\text{success} = \sigma\big((\log h_\text{agent} - \log t_\text{task})\,\beta_\text{agent}\big), \qquad \sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-x}} \]
  • At 80 % success the horizon is ~5× shorter: GPT-5.2 5.9 h at 50 %, 1.1 h at 80 %
  • Wide error bars: Claude Opus 4.6 12 h, 95 % CI 5–60 h. Above 16 h the task suite can't measure reliably
  • The timed humans were new to the code: on four of METR's own pull requests, such contractors took 5–18× longer than its maintainers

T. Kwa et al. (METR), arXiv:2503.14499 v4 (2026), §2–4. Numbers: METR Time Horizon 1.1 data (updated 8 May 2026); 80 % ratio our calculation.

The baseliners: about 5 years of relevant experience on average, most from top-100 universities, working on code new to them. The fit: one logistic curve per agent, in log task length; like item response theory, but the difficulty of a task is the human time, not a fitted parameter. "Agent" = model + METR's scaffold. Tasks: RE-Bench (8-hour ML research engineering), HCAST, and 66 short software actions (SWAA); 170 tasks in the paper, 228 in the Time Horizon 1.1 suite (METR, Jan 2026). Continuously scored tasks are made pass/fail at a human-level threshold. 80 % horizon: we took p80/p50 from METR's benchmark_results_1_1.yaml for the models released 2024–2026: median ratio 5.3, range 4.0–10.3. GPT-5.2: p50 352 min, p80 66 min. Mythos Preview (early): p50 1045 min, p80 186 min, i.e. above 16 h, where METR calls its measurements unreliable. Opus 4.6: p50 719 min (317–3634). External validity, from the paper's §4: (1) the trend reproduces on SWE-bench Verified, with an even shorter doubling time; (2) on tasks scored as "messier" (resource-limited, novel, dynamic environment) the models do worse at a given length, but improve at the same rate: "we find no evidence of plateaus in performance trends specific to the higher messiness subset"; (3) internal pull requests: "contractors taking 5-18x longer to fix issues than repo maintainers", and the agents' performance matched contractor time, not maintainer time. So "12 hours" means 12 hours of a competent person who is new to the code base; for you, in your own code, the same task may be much shorter. The paper's own caveat: "time horizon is always measured relative to a domain, task distribution, and human baseliners' level of skill and context". The paper's extrapolation (v4): if the trend holds and generalizes, 1-month (167 h) software tasks between mid-2028 and mid-2030 (80 % CI), or early 2027 if the 2024–25 trend continues. Present it as their conditional statement, not a prediction of ours. The horizon is a human time; it says nothing about how long the agent itself takes.

Inside the model: attention and the next token

Transformer: the network design of current LLMs. Each token becomes a vector (its embedding); tens to ~100 stacked layers update the vectors. Self-attention maps each vector to a query \(q\), a key \(k\) and a value \(v\).

\[ \mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V, \qquad p(\text{next}=i)=\frac{e^{z_i/T}}{\sum_j e^{z_j/T}} \]
  • Each token averages the values \(v\) of all earlier tokens, weighted by how well its \(q\) matches their \(k\)
  • The last layer scores every vocabulary entry (\(z_i\)). One token is drawn, appended, repeat
  • Temperature \(T\): \(T\to 0\) always picks the most likely token; larger \(T\), more varied text

A. Vaswani et al., arXiv:1706.03762 (2017), eq. (1). Llama 3 405B has 126 layers: A. Grattafiori et al., arXiv:2407.21783 (2024).

This is the whole forward pass, minus the feed-forward (MLP) part of each layer, which holds most of the parameters. \(Q, K, V\) are the matrices of the queries, keys and values of all tokens; \(d_k\) is their length (128 in Llama 3). The \(\sqrt{d_k}\): Vaswani et al. "suspect that for large values of \(d_k\), the dot products grow large in magnitude, pushing the softmax into regions where it has extremely small gradients". "Earlier tokens": in a generating model, a mask hides the later tokens, so the model can't peek at the answer during training. Many heads run in parallel (Llama 3 70B: 64 query heads), each free to learn a different kind of "what to look at". "~100 layers": Llama 3 has 32 (8B), 80 (70B) and 126 (405B) layers. The closed frontier models don't publish their architecture. Sampling: in practice T is combined with cutting off the unlikely tail (top-p or "nucleus" sampling, Holtzman et al., ICLR 2020, arXiv:1904.09751). Likely question: "so it's just autocomplete?" The training objective is next-token prediction, yes; post-training (next backup slide) and tools are what make it an assistant and an agent. Whether "just predicting the next token" limits what it can do is an open debate; the measured abilities on the task-horizon plot are the evidence we can point to.

Why long context is expensive

The KV cache holds the keys and values of all earlier tokens in the GPU's memory, so that they aren't recomputed for every new token.

\[ \frac{\text{KV memory}}{\text{token}} = 2 \times n_\text{layers} \times n_\text{KV heads} \times d_\text{head} \times 2\,\text{B} \overset{\text{Llama 3 70B}}{=} 2\times 80\times 8\times 128\times 2\,\text{B} = 320\,\text{KiB} \]
  • 128k tokens of context: 40 GiB of cache per conversation, next to ~140 GB of model weights
  • Every token attends to every earlier one: the compute for a whole sequence grows as \(n^2\)
  • Hence prompt caching: a re-sent prefix is read back instead of recomputed. Claude: 0.1× the input price (0.05× on Opus 5.5, 0.025× on Fable 5.1). An agent re-sends the whole conversation on every step

Our calculation from the Llama 3 architecture (Table 3 of arXiv:2407.21783), 16-bit numbers. \(n^2\): Vaswani et al., arXiv:1706.03762, Table 1. Prices: Claude API pricing, read 7 Oct 2026.

The factor 2 in front is K and V; the last 2 B is the bytes of one 16-bit (bf16) number. Llama 3 70B: 80 layers, 8 key/value heads, head dimension 8192/64 = 128. 2·80·8·128·2 B = 327 680 B = 320 KiB per token. 128k = 131 072 tokens → 40 GiB (43 GB). Weights: ~70·10⁹ parameters × 2 B ≈ 140 GB. Llama 3 already shrinks this 8-fold with grouped-query attention: 64 query heads share 8 key/value heads ("to reduce the size of key-value caches during decoding", Llama 3 paper §3.2). Servers can also store the cache in 8 bits, halving it. The closed frontier models don't publish their architectures, so their numbers are unknown; the scaling with context length is the point. n²: Vaswani et al. Table 1 gives O(n²·d) per self-attention layer. With the KV cache, each new token costs O(n·d) attention work, but over a whole sequence it's still n². At moderate lengths the feed-forward layers dominate the compute. Our rough estimate for Llama 3 70B: per token and layer, the feed-forward part costs ≈ 2·3·8192·28 672 ≈ 1.4 GFLOP, the attention scores and weighted sum ≈ 4·n·8192 FLOP, equal at n ≈ 40k tokens. Prompt caching: Anthropic doesn't document what exactly it stores; open-source servers such as vLLM (automatic prefix caching) store exactly this KV cache ("caches the KV cache of existing queries, so that a new query can directly reuse the KV cache if it shares the same prefix", vLLM docs). Pricing as of 7 Oct 2026: cache hits are 0.1× the base input price, 0.05× on Opus 5.5 and 0.025× on Fable 5.1; Claude 4.6 and later bill the full 1M-token window at the standard rate. Claude Code's cost docs: "Claude Code sends your full conversation with every request", which is why a long session is mostly cache reads, and why /clear between tasks saves usage.

Honest caveats

How to use AI without being used by it

Mika, ~10 min including Q&A. Showing the downsides is what gives us credibility.

It still makes things up — now at scale

  • Hallucination: confident, plausible, wrong. Citations that don't exist, APIs that don't exist, physics that's off by a factor of 2
  • Sycophancy: it tends to agree with you. Ask for critique explicitly, or let a second agent review
  • The remedy is not trust but verification built into the workflow: tests, validations, cross-checks against known results — things the agent can run itself
  • Session 2 is about exactly this: making your project a place where the agent's mistakes get caught before they reach you
The blackbody exercise later is a hallucination-shaped bug: an exponent of 4 instead of 5. A test catches it in a second.

Security and privacy

  • An agent with shell access is a user on your machine. It can delete, upload, install.
  • Permission modes: Claude Code now starts in auto mode (a safety classifier approves actions); Manual asks before everything; an allow-list lets the safe commands through. Know what each one allows. Auto mode only for work on trusted material, preferably sandboxed
  • Never paste secrets, tokens or personal data into a prompt (unless the model is fully on your own machine). Check your provider's data policy and the university's guidelines

Why models make things up

Singleton rate \(\mathrm{sr}\): the fraction of facts (e.g. birthdays) that appear exactly once in the training data. Kalai et al. prove that for such arbitrary facts the error rate of a calibrated base model on such facts is roughly at least \(\mathrm{sr}\) (up to small correction terms; post-training weakens the bound).

\[ \mathrm{err} \;\gtrsim\; \mathrm{sr}, \qquad \text{score} = \begin{cases} +1 & \text{correct} \\ \;\;\,0 & \text{“I don’t know”} \\ -\tfrac{t}{1-t} & \text{wrong} \end{cases} \]
  • Most benchmarks grade right/wrong only (\(t = 0\)): a guess never scores below "I don't know", so training for them rewards bluffing. With the penalty, answering pays off only above confidence \(t\)
  • Their example: asked for Kalai's birthday "if you know", an open model gave three different wrong dates

A. T. Kalai et al. (OpenAI), Why language models hallucinate, arXiv:2509.04664 (2025), Theorem 2 and §4.2.

Their framing: "Like students facing hard exam questions, large language models sometimes guess when uncertain". Two causes. (1) Pre-training: "Generating valid outputs is in some sense harder than answering these Yes/No questions" (is this output valid?), so the errors of a classifier carry over to generation: they arise from plain statistics, even with error-free training data. For arbitrary facts with no pattern (birthdays), Theorem 2: err ≥ sr − 2/min|E_c| − (35 + 6 ln N)/√N − δ, with probability ≥ 99 %, where δ is a calibration term; the "≳" on the slide hides these correction terms. Their example in words: "if 20% of birthday facts appear exactly once in the pretraining data, then one expects base models to hallucinate on at least 20% of birthday facts." The singleton rate is Turing's "missing mass" estimator (Good 1953). (2) Post-training and evaluation: most benchmarks and leaderboards use binary 0–1 grading, under which "abstaining is strictly sub-optimal". Their proposed fix is socio-technical: put explicit confidence targets into the mainstream benchmarks' instructions ("Answer only if you are > t confident, since mistakes are penalized t/(1 − t) points…"), t = 0.5, 0.75, 0.9 → penalties 1, 3, 9 (the paper prints 2 for t = 0.75, a typo: t/(1 − t) = 3). Expected score of answering with confidence p: p − (1−p)·t/(1−t), positive exactly when p > t. Check: p = t gives t − (1−t)·t/(1−t) = 0. The birthday example: "a state-of-the-art open-source language model output three incorrect dates: '03-07', '15-06', and '01-01', even though a response was requested only if known" (DeepSeek-V3 via the DeepSeek app, 11 May 2025). Measured today: on SimpleQA Verified (1000 short fact questions, Google 2025), the best models in Epoch AI's runs answer ~70–76 % correct when prompted to always give an answer, so the rest are wrong guesses rather than abstentionsly (GPT-6 Astra 75.6 %, Sep 2026; whether tools were allowed isn't stated). So a quarter of plain fact questions still go unanswered or wrong. Practical consequence for us: ask the model to say when it doesn't know, give it a way to check (search, the paper's PDF, a test), and verify citations; a reference that appeared rarely in the training data is exactly the singleton case of the theorem (our reading, not the paper's claim). Sycophancy is a separate failure (agreeing with the user); not covered by this paper.

Hooks: deterministic, the model can't skip them

A hook is a command that Claude Code runs automatically at a fixed moment, e.g. after every file edit. It runs every time; the model can't forget or skip it.


	# PostToolUse hook: lint the file Claude just edited
	import json, subprocess, sys

	path = json.load(sys.stdin)["tool_input"].get("file_path", "")
	if path.endswith(".py"):
		result = subprocess.run(
			[sys.executable, "-m", "ruff", "check", path],
			capture_output=True, text=True)
		if result.returncode != 0:
			print(result.stdout, file=sys.stderr)
			sys.exit(2)  # 2 = tell the agent
  • Events: PreToolUse, PostToolUse, Stop, SessionStart, notifications, …
  • The hook gets the tool call as JSON (structured text) on its standard input; exit code 2 sends its error output back to the agent
  • Use for: formatting, linting, secret scanning, notifications, "don't stop until the tests pass"
  • Antti's research setups use hooks for what must never be skipped: pushes only where approved, Mathematica only with a kill timeout, LaTeX checked after every edit (showcase)

MCP: the standard plug for tools and data

An MCP server is a small program that gives the agent new tools, e.g. searching arXiv or your Zotero library. MCP (Model Context Protocol) is the standard they all follow.


claude mcp list
claude mcp add arxiv -- uvx arxiv-mcp-server
# zotero-mcp must be installed first: see exercise 08
claude mcp add --scope project zotero -- zotero-mcp

These are terminal commands. A project-scope server is written to .mcp.json, which the VS Code extension reads too.

Then, in a session:

"Find the abstract of arXiv:1909.10040 and summarize the sound shell model."

  • Model Context Protocol: a server exposes tools; the agent decides when to call them
  • Servers exist for arXiv, Zotero, GitHub, databases, Slack, lab instruments, your wiki, …
  • Trust: the server runs on your machine and its output enters the context. Prompt injection through a paper abstract is a real thing
  • --scope project writes .mcp.json: the whole group shares it

MCP in practice: one real tool call

Measured in this repository: Claude Code asks the Inkscape server for the size of the workshop poster. The model only writes a request, Claude Code does the talking, and the server runs the real program.

Walk through it top to bottom. Host and client: Claude Code is the host, and it keeps one client connection per server. Server: inkscape-mcp is an ordinary Python program (~12,700 lines), installed as one entry in ~/.claude.json: the command that starts it. Transport: stdio, i.e. Claude Code starts the process and they exchange JSON-RPC messages through its stdin and stdout. Remote servers (claude.ai connectors) use HTTP instead. Discovery: tools/list returns 8 tools, each with a name, a description and a JSON Schema of its arguments; the server's instructions go into the system prompt. Deferred loading: only the tool names are in the context at first; the model loads the full schema with ToolSearch when it needs the tool. The call: the model writes the request, Claude Code checks the permissions and sends tools/call. The server reads the page size from the SVG's viewBox and runs "inkscape --query-width" and "--query-height" as subprocesses for the drawing size. 0.53 s in total. The result goes into the context as text, like the output of any other tool. That's also why prompt injection through MCP output works.

Beyond the editor: worktrees, headless, CI, plugins

  • Git worktree: a second working copy of the same repository in another folder, on its own branch. Several sessions can then work in parallel without stepping on each other (claude --worktree)
  • Headless: Claude Code without the interactive window, from a script or a scheduled job: claude -p "summarize the failing tests"
  • CI (continuous integration): a server that runs your checks on every change you push. Claude Code can run there too, e.g. as a GitHub Action that reviews pull requests
  • Plugin: skills, agents and hooks bundled so that you install them in one go. A whole configuration can also live in a plain repository with an install script, e.g. claude-loadout
  • /insights (terminal version): what have I been asking for repeatedly? → turn it into a skill

No need to write everything yourself; start from a community loadout and edit it to your taste.

  • On a web-research benchmark, the tokens spent alone explained 80 % of the variance in performance

Anthropic, How we built our multi-agent research system (13 Jun 2025). Claude Code docs, Manage costs effectively, read 7 Oct 2026.

All numbers on the slide are Anthropic's, from one system (their Research feature) in June 2025; we haven't seen independent replications. Treat them as orders of magnitude. Quotes: agents use "about 4× more tokens than chat interactions, and multi-agent systems use about 15× more tokens than chats." "a multi-agent system with Claude Opus 4 as the lead agent and Claude Sonnet 4 subagents outperformed single-agent Claude Opus 4 by 90.2% on our internal research eval." BrowseComp (a web-browsing benchmark of hard-to-find information): "token usage by itself explains 80% of the variance, with the number of tool calls and the model choice as the two other explanatory factors." Their own conclusion: "Multi-agent systems work mainly because they help spend enough tokens to solve the problem." Limits, their words: "some domains that require all agents to share the same context or involve many dependencies between agents are not a good fit for multi-agent systems today. For instance, most coding tasks involve fewer truly parallelizable tasks than research." Claude Code's own docs (Oct 2026): agent teams use "approximately 7x more tokens than standard sessions when teammates run in plan mode"; each teammate has its own context window; "Use Sonnet for teammates" to keep cost down. Practical rule for the hands-on: use a subagent when its job is "read a lot, report a little" (exploring a code base, reviewing), so the reading stays out of your main context; don't split one tightly coupled edit across agents. In our measured session (the cost backup in session 1), the subagents made 290 of the 463 requests and about 44 % of the input-side cost (12.3 of 27.6 USD at list price; our calculation from the transcript's usage fields, output excluded because those counts are incomplete).

The agent needs a feedback signal

  • Without tools, an agent can only believe its code works. With tools, it can check.
  • Everything you'd do for reproducible research code helps twice as much now:
